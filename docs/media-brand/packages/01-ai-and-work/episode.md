# When AI changes the work

Production draft for a 15–19 minute YouTube episode. Source: [The Last Human Workforce: Task Bundles, Automation, and Transition Design](https://independentobserver.org/research/the-last-human-workforce/), Volume IV, released web adaptation v2 (9 October 2026). Narration below is original editorial commentary. Bracketed cues are production directions. Use the source link in the video description and captions. Read at about 112–118 words/minute; the two walkthroughs add about 4½ minutes of screen time with the explanatory cards and prompts specified below.

### Opening · two shifts with the same title

[Draw two identical employee badges, then two different timelines. Mark the example hypothetical.]

At the end of a shift, two customer support workers may carry the same job title and do quite different work. One finds an account, looks up the relevant policy, writes the answer, and decides whether the case needs someone senior. The other starts with a suggested answer and spends more time checking whether it fits an unusual case. The employer may say it has introduced AI. The payroll system may say nothing has changed. Both records can be true.

This episode asks what a job title conceals. We'll work through a shift, look at one workplace study, and build a short list of things to measure before calling a transition a success. Keep the three questions separate: What work moves? What new work appears? Who gains the power to decide how it is done?

### A job is a bundle

[Lay out cards labelled look up, diagnose, draft, verify, escalate.]

Economists Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo describe production in terms of tasks. In their framework, a machine can take on an activity formerly done by a person. That can reduce demand for labour in that activity. New tasks can also emerge in which people have an advantage. The two forces can pull in opposite directions. The framework helps explain why a list of software capabilities is an incomplete forecast of employment.

Take the draft card. If a tool produces a first version of a reply, it can save typing. But a person still has to decide whether the reply applies. In straightforward cases that review may be brief. In a case with inconsistent records it may be the longest step. The tool can also create new work: maintaining an approved reference base, testing examples, recording exceptions and finding out why an answer was wrong. Some of that work will be visible in the support role. Some may be moved to a separate team. You have to ask where it went.

A researcher could face the same kind of change. Searching for ideas and producing a rough summary may become easier while tracing a claim back to a reliable edition remains demanding. A teacher might get help generating a practice problem, then spend time checking if the problem teaches the intended concept. These are illustrative possibilities. An occupation contains many institutions, and the same tool can change their work in different ways.

### Walkthrough 1 · where the minutes go

[Allow 2:10: 20 seconds for the first table, 35 seconds for the viewer to mark the likely transferred step, 45 seconds for an animated ordinary case and exception side by side, 30 seconds for the conclusion card. Carry captions through the animation. No measured minutes are implied.]

Here is an example you can do on paper. Write down the five cards. Next to each, mark who does it on Monday before a tool arrives. On a second line, mark who prepares the action, who checks it and who owns a mistake after the tool arrives. Keep those three columns separate. The person preparing the answer need not be the person who can stop it.

Now introduce an exception. The account record says the item was delivered. The customer says it went to a different address. A suggested reply that simply repeats the record has not resolved the disagreement. Somebody needs to find out what evidence exists and who can authorize a correction. If the new system sends the draft immediately, the error may be fast. If a human can examine it, the same tool may still help with the parts that are routine. The exercise doesn't tell us which workflow is best. It tells us what to look for when measuring the one an employer actually uses.

### What was measured

[Source card: Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, final QJE article, 2025; show metric and setting together.]

A study by Erik Brynjolfsson, Danielle Li and Lindsey Raymond gives this discussion a useful reference point. The final journal article examines the introduction of an AI assistant using data from 5,172 customer support agents. It reports an average increase of 15 percent in issues resolved per hour. The people with less experience or lower measured skill saw larger improvements. For the most experienced and highest skilled, speed gains were small and quality declined slightly.

The word average is doing work here. If you manage a mixed team and report one figure, you can miss that the assistance changes how different workers perform. The unit is also important: issues resolved per hour. That tells us something about productivity as defined in this setting. It does not count jobs across an economy. It does not by itself tell us whether the worker receives higher pay, whether an employer increases staffing, or what happens when the tool is used for a different service. Those are separate questions, with different evidence.

There is a reason to pay attention to this result without stretching it. A shift can genuinely get easier for some workers. Finding and applying useful advice sooner may help people who haven't seen hundreds of similar cases. The gains can be valuable even if experienced workers see much less benefit. An employer interested in the result should test where that difference comes from, and should not treat a general target for faster work as a substitute for checking accuracy.

### A serious objection

[Put “demand”, “new tasks”, and “job quality” on separate cards.]

A common response is that a faster service may make a business more competitive, attract new customers, and support more work. That is a good objection to a simple displacement story. Customers might come back sooner because problems are resolved more reliably. The team might offer a service it could not afford before. New roles may emerge around implementation, training or quality control. It would be a mistake to declare these outcomes impossible.

It would also be a mistake to assume they happen automatically. Is there new demand? Are new tasks stable enough to support jobs? Can current staff learn them, or will the new team be hired elsewhere? Does the business share gains in pay, time, training, or greater discretion? There can be a good business outcome and a difficult transition for particular people. Keeping those outcomes separate makes room to find out which one occurred.

### Walkthrough 2 · design a fair test

[Allow 2:20: 25 seconds for the scenario and blank scorecard, 35 seconds for viewers to choose one metric per column, 50 seconds to reveal the measures and comparison, 30 seconds for the takeaway. Show text and icon labels rather than colour alone.]

Imagine an employer tries a tool in one support queue. Before the trial, record the cases each queue handles and who is doing the work. During it, track resolution time, reopenings and the complexity of the cases being routed. Ask how much time people spend checking advice and whether they can override it. Record how training and shifts change. Ask employees whether exceptions are being handled better. Compare with a similar queue where the trial has not started. If the difficult cases all move to the second queue, a straight comparison will mislead.

Now write down a result that would change your mind. For an optimist it might be faster initial answers and more reopened complaints. For a sceptic it might be better quality, greater discretion for new workers and no deterioration for experienced staff. Neither outcome should be edited out of the scorecard. Report differences by experience when the sample supports it, with privacy protections. The goal is a pilot that can tell you where the deployment works and where it needs to change.

### What a transition can hide

[Put a learning curve next to a shift schedule. No data scale.]

Training needs a place in the working week. Consider a new worker who can follow a suggestion successfully and an experienced worker who can explain why the suggestion sometimes fails. The first may benefit from examples; the second may be doing difficult review that the productivity report does not recognize. If the business gives both people the same target, it may reward speed while making the harder contribution invisible. Who has time to document an exception, and who gets to use what the team learned?

There is a second question about mobility. If routine work declines, a course is useful only if it connects someone to a role they can plausibly enter. A pilot could report who was offered training, who could attend during paid hours, and what work was available afterward. It could check whether the system helps people build knowledge they retain when the tool changes. Those measures would tell us something different from a rise in issues resolved per hour. The article's emphasis on transition design is a reason to collect both kinds of evidence.

### Closing · who owns the transition?

[Show four lines: tasks, outcomes, training, distribution.]

The article's policy suggestions follow from that test. Give workers access to training, disclose which tasks are changing and set an evaluation that reflects actual outcomes. These are proposals. A field study can give you a reason to investigate them; it cannot decide the terms of a labour agreement or tell a school what to teach in every context.

The next time you hear that a job will survive or disappear, ask for the task list. Ask what becomes faster, what has to be checked, who can reverse a decision, and where the gains go. If the answers are missing, you have a better question to take back to the evidence. The full Independent Observer article and both sources are linked below.
