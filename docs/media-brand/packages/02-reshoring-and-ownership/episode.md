# The factory is back. Are the jobs?

Production draft for a 15–19 minute YouTube episode. Source: [Factories Return but Do the Jobs](https://independentobserver.org/research/factories-return-but-do-the-jobs/), Volume IV, released web edition v1 from revision 2 (9 October 2026). Use the source link in the description. Read at about 112–118 words/minute; two worked examples add 4½ minutes of instructional screen time.

### A ribbon and three scorecards

[Open on an original drawing of a factory entrance. Overlay “capacity”, “work”, “distribution”.]

Imagine a new factory opening near a town that used to make the same kind of product. There is a ribbon, there are new machines, and local officials point to the return of an industry. It is reasonable to ask what the plant makes and what disruptions a domestic supply can withstand. It is also reasonable for people in town to ask what work will be there when construction ends and whether the earnings can support a household. The answers don't necessarily move together.

This episode takes one phrase, bringing industry home, and splits it into three claims. First, has the country gained productive capacity? Second, have workers gained durable opportunities? Third, where does the income from the additional production go? These are questions an industrial policy should be able to answer separately. If the first is all that was promised, evaluate it on that basis. If jobs were promised too, look at the jobs.

### Walkthrough 1 · output and hours

[Allow 2:00: show the baseline index for 20 seconds; reveal the two factors with a 35-second viewer calculation; animate the multiplication and alternate scenarios for 45 seconds; leave the assumptions card for 20 seconds.]

The article uses a deliberately hypothetical example. Set output at one hundred units, and set labour required per unit at one hour. That's one hundred total hours. Now let output rise twenty percent, so the plant makes a hundred and twenty units. Let labour per unit fall thirty percent, to seven tenths of an hour. Multiply the two. The new total is eighty-four hours. Under those assumptions, output goes up while hours go down.

The numbers are an illustration, not a plant record, and hours are not the same as headcount. The example holds task mix and supplier effects fixed. If the plant sells much more, adds maintenance work or orders more components from local firms, the result could go the other way. If hours are concentrated among fewer workers, a rise in total hours also does not guarantee that more households benefit. This calculation shows why the production number cannot settle the question.

### Employers have more than one response

[Draw separate arrows from a recruitment constraint to hiring, training, automation, an affiliate, and fewer orders.]

Now imagine a different change. An employer finds one recruitment channel less predictable or more costly. It could raise wages to attract workers nearby. It might train a different applicant, change what the job involves, invest in machines or move some activity abroad. It might decide not to take on a new order. Some firms have more of these options than others.

Britta Glennon's study of historical H-1B restrictions examines US multinationals. It finds that affected firms increased employment at foreign affiliates, with existing international capabilities relevant to the response. That's evidence that recruitment restrictions can shift some work overseas in that setting. It does not tell us what a small firm without affiliates will do, or what every visa decision causes. It also doesn't transfer automatically to a different programme or a later change in policy. The study helps us make a list of responses to test.

On the automation side, the comparison should be between completed tasks of acceptable quality. A machine can make a first pass fast and still require someone to check it, keep the process secure and handle failures. An experienced worker may be able to manage more machines or deal with a new type of order. A credible cost comparison includes integration and errors as well as the advertised price of a tool. The question is what the plant actually does differently, and what those differences cost.

### Who receives the gains?

[Three rows: operating jobs and hours; compensation; ownership and value added.]

A local plant can earn more while the labour share of value added goes down. That ratio concerns how the value created is divided. A smaller share of a larger total can coexist with higher real compensation. The share, the total and the experience of different groups are all relevant. Ask whether reported jobs include contractors, whether pay keeps up with prices, whether employees can train for newly important roles, and whether the business still buys from local suppliers.

Research by David Autor and co-authors is consistent with shifts in sales toward firms with lower labour shares contributing to the aggregate decline in labour's share. That finding gives us a reason to investigate concentration and distribution together. It doesn't show that any given large firm misbehaves or that its owners control government. If political influence is alleged, that claim requires its own evidence. For this article the practical step is to name the owners of the equipment, contracts or software and document the arrangements that determine who receives income.

### The strongest case for a more automated plant

[Show two possible alternatives, “plant with fewer jobs” and “no plant”, then a third, “different design”.]

It is tempting to compare a modern plant only with a large employer from decades ago. But if automation is what makes a domestic plant viable, the alternative might be no domestic plant. A project can preserve suppliers and improve reliability with a smaller staff. A restriction aimed at remedying an actual labour violation can also be justified on grounds distinct from jobs. A fair evaluation asks what would have happened without the particular intervention, not what we wish the alternative had been.

The same discipline applies to optimism. Compare with a different viable design if there was one. A project that depended on a subsidy may have been located differently without it, or might have gone ahead anyway. A national demand boom can lift output in supported and unsupported plants alike. The person claiming an effect needs to show why the comparison separates the policy from these other events.

### Walkthrough 2 · build a comparison that can fail

[Allow 2:30: 30 seconds for a blank before/after grid, 40 seconds for viewers to choose a comparison, 50 seconds to annotate demand and timing, 30 seconds to show outcomes that would change the conclusion.]

Imagine a subsidy is offered at the same time that some firms face a recruitment change. Put the dates in separate columns. Find comparable employers who were not affected by each change and look at how their outcomes were already moving. If one group had falling orders before the policy and the other had rising orders, do not erase the difference by starting the chart at the announcement.

On the grid, enter operating hours, real compensation, retention, output, and domestic value added. Give separate lines to construction work, contractor hours, training, and foreign-affiliate employment where you can observe it. Underline the outcomes that support different goals. Sustained gains in work and real pay relative to a plausible comparison would weaken the claim that the policy mainly moved productive assets. Rising output paired with fewer local hours or new overseas work would raise a different question. Neither pattern tells you what an official privately intended.

### Put a date on each promise

[Separate the announcement, construction, and operating years on a timeline.]

An announcement is a statement of intent. A construction contract tells you that work is happening now. An operating report tells you what remains after the equipment is installed. Keep the three records separate. A construction project can pay well and support useful apprenticeships. That matters, but it doesn't answer how many people will maintain the plant or what happens when a supplier loses an order. Ask the publisher of a jobs number which stage it describes and whether it counts people, positions or hours.

It also helps to read local and national results together. A town can gain jobs while another place loses activity. A country can gain control of a critical supply without every component being made nearby. Draw a simple map of where the inputs, work and ownership sit. That map won't settle whether public support was worthwhile. It does show which community is receiving each benefit and where you need a separate measure. This is especially useful when an announcement combines several projects into one headline total.

### Closing · define what the public bought

[Return to three scorecards. Link to the article's proposed test.]

The article proposes that public support be tied, where feasible, to transparent reporting and enforceable benefits. There is room for disagreement about which benefits should count and what reporting is practical. But we can ask a simple question of every claim: which of the three scorecards does this evidence belong to?

A nation can gain resilience, workers can gain durable income, and a community can capture some of the value. Those goals can coincide. Each deserves evidence of its own. The source article contains the full comparison, limitations, and references.
