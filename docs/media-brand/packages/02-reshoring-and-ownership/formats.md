## Website introduction — When factories return, do jobs return too?

[Read the released article and its sources](https://independentobserver.org/research/factories-return-but-do-the-jobs/#article-sources).

A plant opening can create something a country needs even when employment grows less than expected. Separate the promises: productive capacity, sustained work, and the distribution of income. A plant can produce more with fewer hours per unit; whether total hours rise depends on the scale of demand and on new work inside and outside the plant. [Glennon’s study](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2023.4715) illustrates another response to a different policy: some affected multinationals increased foreign-affiliate employment under historical recruitment restrictions. That finding cannot predict the outcome of a particular factory opening. The essay proposes an evaluation that counts operating jobs, real compensation, and domestic value added alongside output. A short version is available on the source page.

## Short 1 — Output can rise as hours fall (30–40 seconds)

[Source for caption](https://independentobserver.org/research/factories-return-but-do-the-jobs/#cost-and-quality). Visual: simple equation with permanent “hypothetical” badge.

“Here’s a worked example, not a factory report. If output rises 20 percent and hours per unit fall 30 percent, multiply 1.2 by 0.7. That gives 0.84, or 16 percent fewer hours. More demand or new tasks could change the result. The point is to count output and work separately. The article in the caption explains what else to look for.”

## Short 2 — Which jobs did the announcement count? (25–35 seconds)

[Source for caption](https://independentobserver.org/research/factories-return-but-do-the-jobs/#test-the-promise). Visual: timeline with distinct construction and operations bars.

“When a plant opens, ask when the promised jobs start and how long they last. Construction and operating work are both real, but they answer different questions. Then ask whether the number includes contractor hours and whether real pay rose. The linked analysis offers a scorecard that keeps those measures separate.”

## Short 3 — Ask who gets the gain (25–35 seconds)

[Source for caption](https://independentobserver.org/research/factories-return-but-do-the-jobs/#ownership-and-gains). Visual: a ledger with distinct “total pay” and “share of value added” rows.

“If a plant creates more value, workers may earn more even while their share of that value falls. The share and the amount are different measurements. Ask about pay after inflation, sustained hours, and who owns the contracts and equipment. The article explains how to test distribution claims without inferring anyone’s motives.”

## LinkedIn post

One announcement can contain three different claims: a country has gained productive capacity, a community has gained durable work, and workers will share in the gains.

Each claim needs its own evidence. Operating hours and real compensation belong beside output and domestic value added. Construction jobs should be counted separately. And a smaller automated plant may still be worthwhile if the credible alternative is no local plant at all.

[Factories Return but Do the Jobs](https://independentobserver.org/research/factories-return-but-do-the-jobs/) offers a way to compare those outcomes and shows why evidence from historical recruitment restrictions has limits.

## The Observer Brief

**Subject:** What should a factory opening be measured against?

**Preview:** A scorecard for production, durable work and ownership.

A new factory can add capacity without restoring an earlier pattern of employment. This reading explains why more output and fewer hours can coexist, and why that outcome does not by itself tell us whether an investment was worthwhile. Look for actual operating jobs, real wages and domestic value added; compare them with a plausible alternative. The article distinguishes its hypothetical example from the historical studies it cites.
